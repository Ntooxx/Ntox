# Cash Flow Forecast

## Triggers
- "cash flow forecast"
- "runway calculation"
- "13 week cash flow"
- "when do we run out of money"
- "will we be short next month"
- "liquidity forecast"
- "burn rate check"

## Purpose
Builds a rolling short-horizon cash flow forecast (13-week for businesses, 12-week for households) that answers one question: on what date does cash go negative, and which lever moves that date?

## Voices
plain numbers, blunt, scenario-driven

## Core Principle

Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact. Forecast cash on the date it actually moves — invoiced is not paid, accrued is not spent — and always model the downside scenario first.

## Workflow

1. Start with the bank balance today (not the accounting balance).
2. Lay out opening cash, week by week, for the chosen horizon.
3. Inflows only when money can arrive that week: adjust expected dates for payment terms and historical collection delays.
4. Outflows when cash leaves: payroll dates, rent, debt service, taxes, scheduled vendors.
5. Add the weekly net and the running closing balance; mark the first negative week in red.
6. Compute runway: closing cash divided by average weekly net outflow.
7. Model three scenarios: base, downside (collections slip 50%, largest customer zero), upside (one-time win lands).
8. Run the levers: what does pushing payment 15 days, accelerating receivables, or pausing one hire do to the negative week?

## Inflow Discipline

- Only count inflows you can defend with a date and a reason; "they usually pay" gets historical delay applied.
- Concentration check: if one customer is more than 30% of inflows, their slip is your crisis — say so.
- Keep a separate line for conditional cash (funding, tax refunds) so the base forecast stays honest.

## Output Format

Lead with: runway in weeks and the first at-risk week. Then the table, then the three levers ranked by days of runway each buys, then the trigger point at which the plan must change (a date, not a feeling).
